\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
In the context of the GW study, there are mainly two possible paths: the first is the \textbf{analysis of existing data} from experiments such as LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA ({\cite{LIGO_cit}},~\cite{Virgo_cit},~\cite{KAGRA}), with the purpose of detecting and characterizing the observable sources within their relative frequency bands;
the second is the \textbf{forecasting approach}, which aims to characterize future experiments in order to better understand what types of sources they could be able to detect and how well.
\vspace{2mm}\\
One of the most important future detectors is LISA (\cite{LISA_ref}), a NASA-ESA collaboration space-based GW detector which is scheduled to launch around 2030s (\cite{Luo_2016}). 
LISA will be arranged as an equilateral triangle with 2.5 million kilometers long arms, placed in a heliocentric orbit, and will operate within the frequency range from approximately $0.1\mathrm{mHz}$ to $1\mathrm{Hz}$ (\cite{baker2019laserinterferometerspaceantenna}).
Among the typical sources that emit GW signal in this range are the \textbf{Compact Binary Coalescences} (\textbf{CBCs}), and in our particular case we are interested in \textbf{Double White Dwarfs} (\textbf{DWDs}).
\vspace{2mm}\\
Many studies have been made to explore the effects of different types of sources on the LISA's sensitivity curve.
For example, we know that the unresolved binary Galactic systems produce a confusion GW foreground that shapes noticeably the curve close to its lowest threshold.
While the Galactic contribution has been extensively investigated, less attention has been devoted to the possible extragalactic binaries foreground.
Therefore, as a natural continuation, the purpose of this work is to estimate the GW foreground produced by the \textit{extragalactic} DWDs in the nearby universe to an extension of $\sim100$Mpc, using {the publicly available code} \underline{\textbf{\href{https://cosmic-popsynth.github.io/docs/stable/pages/about.html}{COSMIC}}} (\cite{Breivik}) to generate synthetic astrophysical populations and the galaxies listed in the \textbf{Gravitational Wave Galaxy Catalog} (\textbf{GWGC},~\cite{GWGC}), an observational survey, as a reference.
The potential impact of these sources in the tentative to characterize the expected LISA sensitivity and to avoid misinterpreting unresolved signals, are the factors that make this research so important.
\vspace{2mm}
\\  
In \textbf{Chapter 1: Theoretical Framework} we give a broad theoretical astrophysical framework, briefly discussing the birth and evolution of DWDs, both as single objects, and in binary systems, and the galactic environments that will host them.
We also introduce the theoretical foundations of GW, starting from the linearized Einstein field equations, to derive the amplitude of the signal generated by an inspiralling binary system, and define the most important parameters.
We then give a brief overview of how GW detectors work, to better understand what sources LISA will be able to see, what resolution and what sensitivity it will have and why in particular we are interested in DWDs.
\vspace{2mm}\\
In \textbf{Chapter 2: Methods and Data} we introduce the concept of stellar population synthesis and the code COSMIC used for this purpose.
We present its features, main parameters, and pipeline, and explain how it is used to generate full-size astrophysical populations of DWDs.
In order to do this, we introduce the GWGC, and explain the procedure to generate the actual fixed populations, scale them to astrophysical full-size galaxies, and compute their GW signal. 
Moreover, we discuss how to take care of the lack of galaxies due to the catalog's incompleteness and the Milky Way coverage in the \textbf{Zone Of Avoidance} (\textbf{ZOA}).
Finally, we discuss the LISA frequency resolution problem, and how it affects the way we must process the GW signals simulated.\vspace{2mm}\\
In \textbf{Chapter 3: Results and Discussion} {we forecast the total resulting GW signal from extragalactic DWDs, and we demonstrate that it is one order of magnitude below LISA's sensitivity curve (\cite{Robson_2019})}.
Therefore, our results indicate that this foreground will not be within LISA's sensitivity range.\vspace{2mm}\\
Finally, in \textbf{Chapter 4: Conclusions and Future Perspectives} we recap our work as a whole, discussing limitations, assumptions, possible extensions and follow-ups.
